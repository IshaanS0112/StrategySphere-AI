# Does the GE-McKinsey quadrant predict anything?

**Short answer: on the panels I built, no — and where the result is significant,
it points the wrong way. The framework does not beat a baseline that uses
revenue growth alone.**

V1's README admitted the attractiveness score had never been checked against
real outcomes. V2 built the harness and admitted it had never been run. V3 runs
it, on 2,979 US filers across two three-year windows assembled from SEC XBRL
filings, and publishes the answer it got.

This document was written before I knew whether I would like the result. The
panel was not rebuilt to improve it, which is the thing a permutation test
exists to catch and the thing worth checking me on: both panels and both
scripts are in the repository, and `git log` shows the panel was built once per
window and scored once.

---

## How the panels were built

```bash
export EDGAR_USER_AGENT="Your Name you@example.com"
python backend/scripts/build_edgar_panel.py --scoring-period CY2020 --horizon 3
python backend/scripts/build_edgar_panel.py --scoring-period CY2021 --horizon 3
python backend/scripts/run_edgar_validation.py \
    data/validation/edgar_panel_CY2020_CY2023.json
```

Every company is scored **through the real pipeline** — the same SWOT engine,
the same HHI-derived competitive intensity, the same GE-McKinsey weighted sum
the API serves. Nothing is reimplemented for the backtest, so the panel measures
this system and not a second model that resembles it.

The market-level inputs each company needs are computed from its own SIC sector
inside the dataset: market growth is the sector's median revenue growth, market
size is the sum of its filers' revenue, industry margin is the sector's median
operating margin, and competitive intensity comes from the HHI over sector
revenue shares. Each company's SWOT benchmark is therefore the median of its
actual sector peers — the peer-set path the engine has always preferred over a
static table.

The outcome is **3-year revenue CAGR**, from the scoring period to three years
later, and both ends must resolve through the *same* XBRL revenue tag or the
company is dropped: a post-ASC-606 tag at one end and a legacy tag at the other
measures the taxonomy change rather than the business.

### Reading it

The companies the framework put in `HARVEST_DIVEST` grew **faster** over the
following three years than the ones it put in `SELECTIVE_INVEST` — 16.7% a year
against 10.4% — and the permutation test says that gap is not chance
(p = 0.0005). The rank correlation between position score and outcome is
significantly **negative** (ρ = −0.084, p = 0.0025). The framework's ordering is
not uninformative here; it is inverted.

The mechanism is visible as soon as the panel is split by sector:

| Sector | n | in `HARVEST_DIVEST` | mean CAGR of that group | sector mean CAGR |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| manufacturing | 511 | 127 | 0.107 | 0.093 |
| services | 164 | 51 | 0.193 | 0.143 |
| saas | 148 | 0 | — | 0.143 |
| retail | 135 | 0 | — | 0.080 |
| financials | 93 | 0 | — | 0.077 |
| utilities | 73 | 0 | — | 0.105 |
| transport | 63 | 23 | 0.342 | 0.203 |
| wholesale | 55 | 13 | 0.199 | 0.121 |

`HARVEST_DIVEST` is concentrated in transport, energy and services: the sectors
that were destroyed in calendar 2020 and rebounded hard through 2021-2023.
Energy's sector median operating margin in CY2020 was **−33%**. The framework
looked at that and said divest, which is exactly what the framework is designed
to say, and the subsequent three years were an oil-price and freight-rate
recovery.

**This is mean reversion off a shock year, and the framework walked straight
into it.** That is a real limitation of the framework as scored on point-in-time
filings, not an artifact I can wave away: the matrix reads a single year's
margins and growth as if they described a steady state.

Note also that the baseline is *more* strongly inverted than the model
(ρ = −0.103 against −0.084). Both are reading the same mean reversion. The
framework is not adding a wrong signal of its own; it is largely re-reading the
one number the baseline uses.

| `SELECTIVE_INVEST` | 1192 | 0.0614 | 0.0557 |
| `HARVEST_DIVEST` | 0 | — | — |

| Model | Spearman rho | permutation p | group separation | p |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GE-McKinsey position | −0.0259 | 0.3123 | −0.0247 | 0.0050 |
| Revenue growth alone | 0.0360 | 0.1624 | 0.0159 | 0.0790 |

### Reading it

The rank correlation is **indistinguishable from chance** (p = 0.31). The group
comparison that is available is significant and again **reversed**: the
companies the framework selected for `INVEST_GROW` grew at 3.7% a year against
6.1% for the middle group (p = 0.005).

The baseline is not significant either on this window (p = 0.16), so this is not
a case of the framework losing to a better predictor. On CY2021 data neither
model predicts the next three years of revenue growth.

## The verdict

**Across two windows and 2,979 company-years, the GE-McKinsey quadrant does not
predict subsequent three-year revenue growth. Where the association reaches
significance it is negative, and the framework never beats revenue growth
used alone.**

I am not going to dress this up. The specific claims:

1. **No window showed the framework predicting in the direction it claims.** Not
   one of the four significance tests came back positive-and-significant.
2. **It does not beat the baseline.** On CY2020 the baseline is a *stronger*
   inverse predictor; on CY2021 neither works. There is no window here where
   knowing the quadrant tells you something revenue growth did not.
3. **The framework is not useless — it is being asked the wrong question.**
   GE-McKinsey was built to allocate capital across units a management team
   already understands, using inputs a management team supplies. Scoring it
   mechanically off one year of XBRL and asking it to forecast revenue is a test
   it was never designed to pass, and this panel does not license the claim that
   a strategy team using it thoughtfully gets nothing.

What it *does* license: **this repository's own output should not be presented
as predictive.** The quadrant is a structured summary of stated position, and
the README now says so.

## Three structural findings about the construction

These cost me more than the p-values did, and they would be the first thing I
would fix.

### 1. The framework never produced all three quadrants

CY2020 produced no `INVEST_GROW`. CY2021 produced no `HARVEST_DIVEST`. Neither
is a coincidence, and it is the most important methodological finding here.

Market inputs are **sector-level**, so every company in a sector shares three of
the four attractiveness terms. Attractiveness is therefore nearly a sector fixed
effect, and a bad year pushes every sector down together while a good year
pushes them all up. CY2020's maximum attractiveness across 1,484 companies was
**3.40**, below the 3.5 threshold — so `INVEST_GROW` was arithmetically
unreachable for every company in the panel.

### 2. HHI over a filer population is always "unconcentrated"

Competitive intensity is derived from the HHI over sector revenue shares. With
dozens of filers per sector and an unnamed residual treated as an atomistic
fringe — a V1 assumption that is documented and biases HHI **down** — every
sector came back `UNCONCENTRATED`, so every company got the maximum rivalry
score of 4.5 and the same 0.3 contribution to its attractiveness. A term that is
constant across the entire panel carries no information, and it costs 0.6 of the
attractiveness axis' range in the process. On a hand-built competitor set of
four or five named rivals this term works as designed; on a population panel it
does not.

### 3. Population-relative benchmarking centres the strength axis

Each company is scored against the median of its own sector peers, so by
construction roughly half the population is above the benchmark on any metric
and half below. The strength axis is therefore centred by construction, and the
framework's ability to discriminate is weaker on a population panel than on the
hand-picked peer sets it is designed for.

## Everything wrong with this test, stated plainly

**Look-ahead bias.** Facts for CY2020 are filed in early 2021, so a score "as at
2020" uses information that was not public until months later. This biases the
result **toward** the model looking good — and it still did not look good.
Filing-date-aware assembly is the fix and is scoped as V4 rather than pretended
away.

**Both windows straddle a structural shock.** CY2020 is a pandemic trough and
CY2021 a rebound. There is no clean recent three-year window available in this
data, and I did not keep searching for one — that search is the exact failure
the permutation test exists to catch.

**Revenue CAGR is one outcome among several.** TSR or margin expansion might
behave differently. I picked revenue growth before running anything, because it
is the outcome the framework most plausibly speaks to and because it is
available from the same source as the inputs.

**Sector shares are shares of SEC filers**, not of real markets. Private and
foreign competitors are absent.

**Survivorship.** A company must report revenue in both the scoring and the
outcome period to enter the panel. Companies that were acquired, delisted or
went bankrupt inside the window are absent — 1,173 of them were dropped for
having no CY2023 revenue. That removes the worst outcomes from the sample and
biases every mean upward, and it hits `HARVEST_DIVEST` hardest, which is the
group most likely to fail outright. If anything, this makes the reported
inversion an understatement.

**n is large but effectively smaller.** 1,484 companies within 15 sectors that
share market inputs is not 1,484 independent observations of the framework.

---

## What would make this a fair test

1. **Filing-date-aware assembly** — score on what was public at the scoring
   date. V4.
2. **Hand-built competitor sets** for a smaller panel, so the HHI term carries
   information instead of being pinned at one value.
3. **A window without a structural shock at one end**, which means going back
   further than the XBRL frames API comfortably reaches.
4. **A second outcome measure** — TSR, or margin expansion — declared before
   the run.

Until those exist, the honest summary is the one at the top of this document.

## Reproducing it

```bash
cd backend && pip install -r requirements-dev.txt
export EDGAR_USER_AGENT="Your Name you@example.com"

python scripts/build_edgar_panel.py --scoring-period CY2020 --horizon 3
python scripts/run_edgar_validation.py \
    ../data/validation/edgar_panel_CY2020_CY2023.json
```

Both panels are committed, so the scoring step can be skipped and the numbers
above checked directly. The permutation tests are seeded
(`VALIDATION_RANDOM_SEED=20260908`), so they reproduce exactly.
